What happens next?

  1. The Central Elections Committee completes certification and publication.
  2. The President consults all elected factions. President Isaac Herzog announced consultations beginning 4 November 2026.
  3. The President assigns an MK the task of forming a government.
  4. The designated MK normally has 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension, under the applicable Basic Law framework.
  5. If no government is formed, another MK may be tasked; failure can lead to another election.
L2 · END-TO-END PROCESS

How an Israeli general election becomes a government

Election day determines parliamentary representation. It does not directly elect the Prime Minister.

Election-to-government lifecycle

1. Election calledThe Knesset term can end at the normal statutory point or early dissolution can trigger an early election.
↓
2. Lists registeredParties and electoral alliances submit candidate lists, subject to election law and eligibility requirements.
↓
3. Nationwide votingEligible voters cast one ballot for a party/list. There are no separate constituency ballots for individual MKs.
↓
4. Count and thresholdValid votes are counted; lists below 3.25% do not receive seats.
↓
5. Seat allocation120 seats are distributed proportionally, with surplus-vote arrangements and the Bader–Ofer allocation method.
↓
6. Presidential consultationsThe President consults elected factions about who is best placed to form a government.
↓
7. Coalition negotiationsParties negotiate policy guidelines, ministerial portfolios, coalition discipline and confidence support.
↓
8. Government presentedThe proposed government and its policy guidelines are presented to the Knesset for confidence.
↓
9. Government takes officeOnce confidence is secured, the new government assumes office. If formation fails, further attempts or a new election may follow.

Key distinction

Israelis elect the Knesset, not the Prime Minister directly. The President does not personally choose a preferred political programme; the President’s task is to assign the formation mandate to an MK assessed as most likely to secure Knesset confidence.

L3 · TECHNICAL MECHANICS

How votes become Knesset seats

Israel uses a single nationwide party-list proportional representation system.

Seat allocation, simplified

  1. Count all valid ballots cast for each list.
  2. Exclude lists that fail the 3.25% electoral threshold.
  3. Calculate the electoral quotient from the valid votes of qualifying lists divided by 120.
  4. Allocate whole seats proportionally using the statutory allocation method.
  5. Allocate remaining seats using the Bader–Ofer method; surplus-vote agreements between lists can affect the final allocation.
  6. Seats are filled by candidates in the order shown on each closed list.

Why coalitions are central

Because many parties can win representation and no one party normally wins 61 seats alone, governments are built from coalition agreements. Small parties can gain bargaining leverage when their seats are needed for a majority. Coalition instability can cause budget disputes, defections, no-confidence motions or early elections.

What voters do not directly choose

  • They do not directly elect the Prime Minister.
  • They do not elect 120 local constituency MPs.
  • They do not vote separately for ministers.
  • They do not elect the President by popular vote; the Knesset elects the President.

VOTING & COUNTING

Voter eligibility, ballots and election administration

National parliamentary elections are conducted as a party-list vote under the Knesset election laws.

Who can vote?

  • Israeli citizens aged 18 or older on election day are generally eligible to vote, subject to statutory requirements.
  • Voting is normally conducted at polling stations in Israel. Special arrangements apply to certain categories, including eligible diplomatic and service personnel.
  • Voters cast one ballot for a registered list; they do not rank candidates or select an individual district representative.

Ballot and counting

  1. Voter receives an official ballot envelope and selects one party/list slip.
  2. The sealed ballot is placed in the ballot box.
  3. Polling station committees count and report results under election administration rules.
  4. Votes are aggregated nationally; threshold eligibility is assessed.
  5. Seat allocation is calculated and results are published by the Central Elections Committee.

Israel uses paper ballots. Digital reporting and administrative systems do not mean that the voter casts a ballot through an internet voting platform.

Central Elections Committee

The Central Elections Committee is responsible for administering and supervising Knesset elections, including candidate/list matters, polling arrangements, counting, and publication of results. Its chair is a Supreme Court justice, while its membership reflects parliamentary representation.

EXECUTIVE BRANCH

President, Prime Minister & Government

Israel is a parliamentary system. The President is head of state; the Prime Minister leads the executive government.

Current officeholders · 4 November 2026

OfficeOfficeholderRole
PresidentIsaac HerzogHead of state; elected by the Knesset for one seven-year term. Consults factions and assigns the government-formation task.
Prime MinisterBenjamin NetanyahuHead of government; remains in office in caretaker/continuity capacity until a successor government is formed and takes office.
The election result does not automatically remove the incumbent Prime Minister. The outgoing government continues until a new government is approved and assumes office.

President of Israel

  • Chosen by the Knesset in a secret ballot.
  • Serves a single seven-year term.
  • Performs constitutional and representative functions, including formal roles in government formation and pardons.
  • Does not run the day-to-day government or command a parliamentary majority.

Prime Minister and Cabinet

  • The Prime Minister leads the government and coordinates ministers and policy.
  • Ministers head government ministries and are collectively responsible to the Knesset.
  • The government requires Knesset confidence and can be replaced through a constructive no-confidence process under the Basic Law: The Government.
  • Coalition agreements are politically important but do not override Basic Laws or ordinary legislation.

Government formation in the current cycle

President Herzog announced that consultations with factions elected to the 26th Knesset begin on 4 November 2026. After consultations, the President will designate an MK to form a government. The announced process gives the designee 28 days, with an extension possible under the Basic Law framework.

LEGISLATIVE BRANCH

The Knesset · Israel’s parliament

One chamber, 120 members, elected through nationwide proportional representation.

What is the Knesset?

  • Unicameral national legislature with 120 Members of Knesset (MKs).
  • Legislates, approves budgets, scrutinises ministers, and expresses or withdraws confidence in governments.
  • Its committees examine bills and government activity.
  • It elects the President and has roles in judicial appointments and other constitutional processes.

How a bill becomes law

  1. Bill introduced by government or MKs.
  2. Preliminary reading may apply to private members’ bills.
  3. Committee deliberation and amendments.
  4. First, second and third readings as required.
  5. Publication and commencement under the law’s provisions.
JUDICIAL BRANCH & CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Courts, Basic Laws & constitutional review

Israel does not have a single consolidated written constitution. Its constitutional framework is built around Basic Laws, ordinary statutes, judicial decisions and foundational declarations.

Basic Laws

The Knesset has adopted Basic Laws covering institutions and rights, including the Knesset, Government, President, Judiciary, State Economy, Human Dignity and Liberty, Freedom of Occupation, and Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People. The Harari Resolution of 1950 envisaged a constitution developed chapter by chapter through Basic Laws.

Court structure

  • Supreme Court: highest court; sits as a court of appeal and as the High Court of Justice (HCJ) in petitions against public authorities.
  • District courts: major trial and appellate jurisdiction.
  • Magistrates’ courts: lower-level civil and criminal matters.
  • Specialised and religious courts: including labour, family and recognised religious jurisdictions within their statutory fields.

Judicial selection and reform debate

Judicial appointments are made through a statutory selection committee system. Proposals to change the committee’s composition, the Court’s review powers and the use of reasonableness review triggered major protests and constitutional controversy in 2023–2024. In January 2024, the Supreme Court struck down the 2023 amendment that restricted the reasonableness doctrine, in a landmark decision. Debates over judicial power, parliamentary sovereignty and checks and balances remain politically consequential.

Important distinction

Israel’s Basic Laws form a constitutional framework, but they are not equivalent to a single entrenched constitution with a uniform amendment procedure. The scope of judicial review and the relationship between the Knesset and Supreme Court remain subjects of legal and political debate.

Civilian control and accountability

The government and Defence Minister exercise civilian authority over the IDF. The Chief of General Staff commands the military under the authority of the government and Defence Minister. Intelligence agencies operate under statutory and executive frameworks, with parliamentary, judicial and other oversight mechanisms whose effectiveness is debated.

Security does not remove legal questions

Military necessity, protection of civilians, hostages, detainees, proportionality, distinction, occupation law and accountability are distinct legal questions. Allegations about specific operations should be attributed to the relevant court, commission, government, UN body or investigative organisation rather than stated as settled findings without evidence.

PEOPLE & INSTITUTIONS

Key leaders, founders, movements & public institutions

Israel’s political history has been shaped by founding leaders, military commanders, diplomats, judges, social movements and minority communities.

Founding and early statehood

  • Theodor Herzl: political Zionism’s principal early theorist and organiser.
  • David Ben-Gurion: led the declaration of independence and early state-building.
  • Chaim Weizmann: scientist and Zionist diplomat; first President.
  • Golda Meir: early labour movement leader and Prime Minister.
  • Menachem Begin: Revisionist Zionist leader; led the 1977 political transition and Egypt peace treaty.

Peace process and modern politics

  • Yitzhak Rabin: Prime Minister and central Oslo Accords figure; assassinated in 1995.
  • Shimon Peres: central figure in diplomacy and the Oslo process.
  • Ariel Sharon: military and political leader; Gaza disengagement architect.
  • Benjamin Netanyahu: dominant political figure across multiple terms since 1996.
  • Isaac Herzog: President from 2021.
  • Gadi Eisenkot: former IDF Chief of Staff and leader of Yashar in the 2026 election.
  • Naftali Bennett: former Prime Minister and leader of B’Yachad in the 2026 election.
  • Yair Golan: former deputy IDF chief and leader of The Democrats.

Institutions and civil society

  • Histadrut: major labour federation with a historic role in the pre-state and early state economy.
  • Jewish Agency: institution central to immigration and Zionist state-building before and after 1948.
  • Supreme Court: central to constitutional and administrative law disputes.
  • Central Elections Committee: election administration and supervision.
  • Local authorities: municipal government, services, planning and local political representation.
  • Arab citizens of Israel: a major minority electorate with diverse political parties and civic organisations.
POLICY, CONFLICT & CONTROVERSY

Major policies, wars, reforms & contested decisions

Separate historical events, official policy, legal findings, allegations and competing interpretations.

State-building, economy and society

  • Mass immigration: large waves of Jewish immigration reshaped population, housing, labour and state institutions.
  • Reparations agreement (1952): West German reparations and restitution became a major political controversy.
  • Economic liberalisation: reforms accelerated in the 1980s and 1990s; Israel developed a high-tech export economy alongside persistent inequality and cost-of-living pressures.
  • Nation-State Basic Law (2018): defined Israel as the nation-state of the Jewish people; critics argued it downgraded equality and minority status, while supporters defended national self-determination.

Wars and territorial developments

  • 1948 Arab–Israeli War: establishment of Israel, war with neighbouring Arab states, Palestinian displacement (Nakba) and armistice lines.
  • 1956 Suez Crisis: Israel joined Britain and France in the campaign against Egypt.
  • 1967 Six-Day War: Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, Sinai and Golan Heights.
  • 1973 Yom Kippur War: Egyptian and Syrian surprise attack; major reassessment of intelligence and preparedness.
  • 1979 Egypt peace treaty: formal peace and Israeli withdrawal from Sinai.
  • 1982 Lebanon War: invasion of Lebanon and long-term consequences; Sabra and Shatila massacre occurred under Israeli control of the area, with the Kahan Commission finding indirect Israeli responsibility and personal responsibility of senior officials in failing to prevent the massacre.
  • 1993–1995 Oslo Accords: mutual recognition between Israel and PLO and creation of Palestinian Authority; final-status issues unresolved.
  • 2005 Gaza disengagement: Israel removed settlements and permanent ground forces from Gaza.
  • 2023–2026 war: Hamas-led 7 October 2023 attacks killed civilians and security personnel and involved hostage-taking; Israel launched a major military campaign in Gaza. The conduct, humanitarian consequences, hostages, displacement, alleged violations and legal proceedings have been the subject of extensive international scrutiny and dispute.

Domestic political controversies

  • Netanyahu corruption trial: Netanyahu faces charges in Cases 1000, 2000 and 4000; he denies wrongdoing. Charges are allegations until adjudicated.
  • Judicial overhaul proposals: 2023 proposals triggered mass protests and a constitutional crisis; the Supreme Court invalidated the reasonableness amendment in January 2024.
  • Ultra-Orthodox military conscription: exemptions and enlistment policy remain major legal, social and coalition issues.
  • Settlement policy: Israeli settlements in occupied West Bank are considered illegal under the prevailing international legal view; Israel disputes this legal interpretation.
  • Gaza humanitarian situation: civilian deaths, displacement, aid access, destruction and accountability are major international concerns; casualty estimates and legal characterisations vary by source and date.

How to assess claims

For every controversy, ask: who made the claim; what evidence is public; whether an official inquiry, court or independent investigation reached a finding; whether the finding is final; and whether the subject disputes it. Do not treat an accusation, an interim legal measure or a political slogan as a final court judgment.

FOREIGN POLICY & INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS

Israel and the world

Foreign relations are shaped by security, diplomacy, trade, technology, energy, religion and the unresolved Israeli–Palestinian conflict.

India–Israel relations

  • Full diplomatic relations established in 1992.
  • Cooperation includes defence, agriculture, water management, innovation, cybersecurity, science and technology.
  • India has historically supported a two-state solution and maintains relations with Israel and Palestine.
  • Defence ties have expanded over time, while India’s public positions on Gaza and Palestinian statehood reflect its broader regional diplomacy.

United States

  • Israel’s most important strategic partner and principal military aid partner.
  • Relations include security assistance, intelligence, diplomacy, technology and domestic political debate in the United States.
  • Differences can arise over settlements, Palestinian statehood, Iran, military operations and civilian protection.

Regional relationships

  • Egypt: peace treaty since 1979.
  • Jordan: peace treaty since 1994.
  • United Arab Emirates, Bahrain and Morocco: normalisation agreements announced in 2020 under the Abraham Accords framework (Morocco’s arrangement has its own diplomatic context).
  • Saudi Arabia: normalisation discussions have been affected by the Gaza war and Palestinian statehood questions.
  • Iran: adversarial relationship involving nuclear programme concerns, regional armed groups and direct military escalation.
  • Lebanon and Syria: security and border issues, with no full peace treaty.

Europe, Russia and China

European states are important trade and diplomatic partners, but differ over settlements, international law and the Gaza war. Russia has maintained contacts with regional actors while relations with Israel have been affected by the Ukraine war and Middle East conflicts. China is a major trade and technology partner, while Israel’s security ties with the United States constrain some strategic technology decisions.

SOCIETY, DEMOGRAPHY & TERRITORY

Population, communities & contested geography

Israel is a diverse society. Population estimates vary with the definition of residents and whether East Jerusalem, the Golan and other territories are included.

Major communities and identities

  • Jewish Israelis, including diverse Ashkenazi, Mizrahi, Sephardi, Ethiopian, Russian-speaking and other communities.
  • Arab citizens of Israel, including Muslim, Christian, Druze and Bedouin communities; these groups are internally diverse.
  • Other communities include Circassians, migrants, asylum seekers and non-citizen residents.
  • Religious identity, language, ethnicity, class, geography and national identity all influence political preferences, but none should be treated as a single uniform voting bloc.

Local government

Israel has municipalities, local councils and regional councils. Local authorities provide services such as education support, sanitation, planning, local roads and community services, within national statutory and budgetary frameworks.

Population and territory: caution

Always identify the statistical boundary used. Israeli statistical releases, UN datasets and international demographic estimates may differ in their treatment of East Jerusalem, the Golan Heights, West Bank settlements and Palestinian residents. Avoid combining figures from incompatible territorial definitions.

FAQs

Does Israel directly elect its Prime Minister?

No. Voters elect the Knesset. The President assigns an MK the task of forming a government, which must obtain Knesset confidence.

Why are there so many parties?

Nationwide proportional representation with a relatively low threshold allows multiple political currents to gain seats. Coalition formation then aggregates parties into governing majorities.

Is Israel’s President like the US President?

No. Israel’s President is a head of state with mostly ceremonial and constitutional functions. Executive power is exercised by the government led by the Prime Minister.

Does Israel have a written constitution?

It has no single consolidated constitution. Basic Laws form a constitutional framework, supplemented by statutes and judicial decisions.

Are the West Bank and Gaza part of Israel’s six administrative districts?

No. They are not among the six districts of Israel’s domestic administrative map. Their territorial and legal status is distinct and contested in international diplomacy and law.
