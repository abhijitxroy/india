A crucial distinction: constitutional design vs political practice

Russia’s Constitution describes a federal republic with elected institutions, separation of powers and a directly elected president. In practice, independent assessments describe a highly centralised political system in which the presidency dominates national decision-making, the ruling party controls a large parliamentary majority, and meaningful opposition and independent media face severe restrictions.

Interpretation rule: do not treat the constitutional text, official election result, independent election assessment and political analysis as interchangeable. This reference labels them separately.

ELECTION SYSTEM

L2 · Full election flow

How a Russian national election is organised in law, from candidate registration to official certification.

National election process

VOTER ELIGIBILITY & REGISTRATION ↓ CANDIDATE NOMINATION / PARTY LISTS ↓ CENTRAL ELECTION COMMISSION (CEC) ↓ CAMPAIGN PERIOD · MEDIA · DEBATES ↓ VOTING (PAPER BALLOTS / ELECTRONIC METHODS IN SOME AREAS) ↓ PRECINCT COUNTING → TERRITORIAL COMMISSIONS ↓ REGIONAL COMMISSIONS → CENTRAL ELECTION COMMISSION ↓ OFFICIAL RESULTS → COURT CHALLENGES / VALIDATION

For presidential elections, the CEC organises the nationwide process. State Duma elections use a parallel mixed system: party lists and single-member districts. Regional elections are administered through regional and territorial election commissions under federal law.

The flow above describes the formal legal process. It does not by itself establish that the electoral environment is competitive, pluralistic or free from coercion.

ELECTION SYSTEM

Voting, ballots, electronic voting & counting

Voting methods, election commissions, ballot secrecy, observation and the distinction between official results and independent assessments.

Voting and counting

  • Russian elections use paper ballots, with electronic voting systems also deployed in selected jurisdictions and election cycles. Remote electronic voting has expanded, especially in large cities and selected regions.
  • Polling stations are administered by precinct election commissions; results are aggregated through territorial and regional commissions before CEC certification.
  • Observers may include registered candidate/party representatives and other legally accredited observers. The legal framework and practical access have changed over time.
  • Official turnout and vote shares are reported by the CEC. Independent organisations and international bodies assess the competitiveness, campaign conditions, media access, candidate exclusion and transparency separately.
  • For the 2024 presidential election, OSCE/ODIHR did not observe the vote because Russia did not invite the mission; the OSCE stated that this was deeply regrettable.
  • For the September 2026 Duma election, the CEC certified a 450-seat result. Independent analysis should consider candidate access, opposition constraints, media conditions, war-time environment and the scope of observation.
A reported vote count is an official administrative result. Whether an election meets democratic standards is a separate question requiring evidence about the full electoral environment.

Regional governance

  • Each federal subject has a regional executive headed by a governor or equivalent senior executive; a regional legislature; and a regional charter or constitution.
  • Governors are generally selected through direct regional elections, but federal law provides for nomination and appointment mechanisms and the president has significant influence over regional leadership.
  • Federal districts (created in 2000) are presidential administrative groupings overseen by presidential plenipotentiary envoys; they are not constitutional federal subjects.
  • Federal law and federal executive institutions have substantial influence over regional budgets, security, appointments and policy implementation.
  • Republics have distinct national-territorial status, but the Constitution also establishes the supremacy of the federal Constitution and federal laws in the constitutional hierarchy.

Constitutional text vs checks in practice

The Constitution formally distributes powers among the President, Federal Assembly, Government and courts. Yet the presidential office, security institutions and a dominant parliamentary majority produce a strong executive-centred system. The existence of a court or legislature does not, by itself, demonstrate effective institutional checks.

Opposition and political pluralism

  • Alexei Navalny and his anti-corruption movement became a major challenge to the Kremlin; Navalny died in prison in February 2024. His organisations were designated extremist by Russian courts in 2021.
  • Boris Nemtsov, a former deputy prime minister and opposition leader, was assassinated in Moscow in 2015. The case produced convictions, while questions about those who ordered the killing have remained a major public issue.
  • Yabloko, independent municipal politicians, anti-corruption groups, independent journalists and anti-war activists have faced restrictions, legal pressure or exile.
  • Communist Party, LDPR, A Just Russia and New People have seats and public profiles, but their parliamentary behaviour often remains within the boundaries of the established political system.
  • Freedom House and Human Rights Watch report restrictions on independent media, assembly, political opposition and anti-war expression. These are independent assessments, not Russian government descriptions.
Avoid calling every parliamentary party an independent opposition force. Distinguish “systemic opposition” (allowed parliamentary parties) from non-systemic opposition movements that challenge the governing system itself.

Important constitutional and election context

  • Boris Yeltsin was elected President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic in June 1991, before the formal dissolution of the USSR in December 1991.
  • The September–October 1993 constitutional crisis ended in armed confrontation between Yeltsin and the legislature; Yeltsin’s forces prevailed, and a new Constitution was adopted by referendum in December 1993.
  • In 2008, constitutional amendments extended the presidential term from four to six years, effective for the next presidential term.
  • In 2020, constitutional amendments included a transitional provision that allowed the sitting president’s prior terms not to count under the amended term-limit wording. This enabled Putin to run again in 2024 and potentially in 2030.
  • Putin’s 2024 official result was 87.28%. OSCE/ODIHR did not observe the election because Russia did not invite the mission; critics raised concerns about candidate restrictions, media environment and political repression.

INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS

Russia & the world · India, USA, China and Europe

Russia’s external relations combine great-power ambitions, security policy, energy exports, sanctions and strategic partnerships.

India–Russia relations

India and Russia maintain a “Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership” established in 2010, building on the 2000 Strategic Partnership declaration. The 23rd Annual Summit was held in New Delhi on 4–5 December 2025. Cooperation includes defence, energy, nuclear power, space, trade, technology, education and multilateral forums.

  • Defence: long-standing supplier relationship, joint production and systems such as BrahMos; India has diversified procurement and expanded domestic manufacturing.
  • Energy: crude oil imports grew substantially after 2022, shaped by price, sanctions and shipping constraints.
  • Diplomacy: India advocates dialogue and diplomacy, maintains strategic autonomy, and balances relations with Russia, the United States, Europe and Indo-Pacific partners.
  • Trade and connectivity: payment mechanisms, logistics, International North–South Transport Corridor and Arctic routes remain policy areas.
  • Risks: sanctions exposure, payment/insurance complications, dependence on selected defence platforms, and geopolitical pressure.

PEOPLE & GEOGRAPHY

Population, territory & federal structure

Russia’s enormous territory, multi-ethnic population and regional diversity shape its federal institutions and security policy.

Territory and population

  • Russia is the world’s largest country by land area, spanning Eastern Europe and northern Asia and eleven time zones.
  • Its population is roughly 146 million by commonly cited recent official estimates; exact figures and coverage are affected by the treatment of occupied/claimed territories and demographic revisions.
  • Russian is the federal state language. Republics may establish additional state languages under the Constitution.
  • Russia is ethnically and linguistically diverse, including Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Chuvash, Avars, Armenians, Ukrainians, Sakha and many other communities.
  • Federal subjects differ greatly in population, economic structure, resource endowment, geography and degree of autonomy.
  • Federal districts are administrative groupings of federal subjects and should not be confused with the constitutional subjects themselves.
Population estimates and territorial totals require date-specific sourcing. Russian official statistics may include territories whose annexation is not internationally recognised; international datasets may use different territorial coverage.

Frequently asked questions

Is Russia a democracy?

Russia’s Constitution describes the state as democratic and provides for elections and multiple institutions. Independent democracy assessments generally classify the current system as authoritarian or an electoral autocracy, citing restrictions on opposition, media, civil society and electoral competition.

Is Russia a presidential or parliamentary system?

It is constitutionally a presidential republic with a government led by a Prime Minister and a legislature. The presidency is substantially stronger than in a typical parliamentary system.

Why is the Duma majority important?

A large pro-government majority can pass legislation and approve executive proposals with limited parliamentary resistance.

Does Russia have 83 or 89 regions?

The internationally recognised territory has 83 federal subjects in many administrative datasets. Russia’s constitutional claim lists 89 after incorporating six Ukrainian territories; those annexations are not internationally recognised.
