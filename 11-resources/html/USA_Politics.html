One country, three levels of government

The United States is a federal presidential republic. Power is constitutionally divided between the federal government and 50 state governments. At the federal level, the three branches are separate: executive, legislative and judicial.

LEVEL 1 · BIG PICTURE

How a US presidential election works

The simple model: political parties choose nominees, voters vote in each state, states appoint electors, electors vote for President and Vice President, and Congress counts those votes.

One-screen process map

What does a US voter usually vote for?

President / Vice President

A presidential ticket. In most states, the statewide result determines which elector slate is appointed.

Congress

A candidate for the House district and, when that Senate seat is up, a US Senator.

State & local offices

Depending on the state and election year: governor, state legislators, judges, mayors, sheriffs, ballot measures and more.

Do not confuse popular vote with Electoral College vote. A candidate can win more votes nationwide and still lose the presidency by failing to win a majority of electoral votes. The nationwide popular vote is not the constitutional tally used to determine the winner.

LEVEL 3 · INTERNAL MECHANICS

How the machinery actually works

The detailed rules behind voters, party primaries, delegates, elector slates, vote counting, contingent elections and federal/state responsibilities.

A. Primaries: who can participate?

Open primary

Depending on state rules, a voter may choose which party's primary to participate in without being registered with that party.

Closed primary

Generally limited to voters registered with that party.

Caucus

A party-run meeting at precinct, county or district level. Some use discussion and grouping; formats vary.

These are broad categories, not a single nationwide rule. State law and party rules determine eligibility, ballot format, delegate allocation and deadlines.

B. Delegates are not Electoral College electors

Party delegates

Participate in a party's national convention and help nominate the party's presidential candidate.

Stage: nomination, before the general election.
Electoral College electors

Cast the constitutionally prescribed votes for President and Vice President after the November general election.

Stage: formal presidential election, after the general election.
Memory aid: Delegates choose the party nominee. Electors choose the President and Vice President under the Electoral College system.

C. How does a state's popular vote turn into electors?

  1. Each party/state political organisation identifies a slate of potential electors pledged to its presidential ticket.
  2. Voters cast ballots for the presidential ticket. The state counts the votes under its election law.
  3. The statewide winner generally has its elector slate appointed in 48 states and Washington, D.C.
  4. Maine and Nebraska use a district-based allocation: two electors go with the statewide result; one elector is tied to each congressional district result.
  5. The appointed electors meet in December and vote separately for President and Vice President.

D. What if no one wins 270 electoral votes?

This is called a contingent election under the 12th Amendment. It is not the ordinary method and has been used rarely.

Electoral College · 50 states · 538 votes

The Electoral College is a constitutional process, not a physical college or a separate legislative chamber. Its 538 electors reflect each state's House seats plus two Senate seats, plus three for Washington, D.C.

Formula: electoral votes per state

State electoral votes = Number of US House seats for that state + 2 Senators

Winner-take-all vs district allocation

48 states + Washington, D.C.

The statewide popular-vote winner's elector slate generally receives all of that jurisdiction's electoral votes.

Maine & Nebraska

Two electors follow the statewide result; the remaining electors are awarded by congressional district result. This can split the state's electoral votes.

Why the nationwide popular-vote winner can lose

Presidential elections are decided by electoral votes, not by adding every ballot into one nationwide total. A candidate may win several large states by narrow margins and lose other states by larger margins, producing fewer electoral votes despite more votes nationwide.

BALLOT TO CERTIFIED RESULT

Voter eligibility, voting methods and counting

There is no single federal polling-day operating manual for every detail. Federal law sets important baselines, while states and local jurisdictions administer most election operations.

Who can vote in federal elections?

  • US citizens who meet the applicable age and registration requirements.
  • Voting age is generally 18; states set registration procedures and deadlines within federal law.
  • Eligibility rules for people with felony convictions vary by state.
  • Non-citizens cannot vote in federal elections. A few local jurisdictions have separately allowed non-citizen voting in certain local elections, subject to law.
Unlike India, voter registration is generally an individual process in the US. Many states require voters to register before voting, although rules and deadlines vary; some states offer same-day registration.

Ways to cast a ballot

Election Day, in person

Vote at the assigned polling place or vote centre.

Early in-person voting

Available in many states before Election Day; timing and access differ.

Absentee / mail ballot

Rules differ: some states broadly mail ballots; others require an excuse or application.

Counting and certification flow

Key distinction: election-night media projections are estimates. Official results follow canvass and certification procedures. Recounts and court challenges can affect timelines.
FEDERAL SYSTEM

Three branches of the US federal government

Separate branches with checks and balances. The President does not sit in Congress, and Congress does not select the President in an ordinary election.

Federal government structure

Executive branch · President

President

Head of state, head of federal executive and Commander in Chief. Four-year term; elected through the Electoral College.

Vice President

First in presidential succession; presides over the Senate and may cast tie-breaking votes.

Cabinet

Heads of executive departments, nominated by the President and generally confirmed by the Senate.

Federal agencies

Administer laws and deliver services within authority delegated by Congress and the executive branch.

Presidential eligibility (constitutional)

  • Natural-born US citizen.
  • At least 35 years old.
  • US resident for at least 14 years.

The 22nd Amendment generally bars a person from being elected President more than twice.

Judicial branch

The federal judiciary includes the Supreme Court, Courts of Appeals and District Courts. Federal judges are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate. They generally hold office during good behaviour, subject to constitutional removal mechanisms.

The Supreme Court is not an elected chamber. It decides cases and controversies and can review the constitutionality of government action.

ARTICLE I · LEGISLATIVE BRANCH

Congress · House of Representatives & Senate

Congress is bicameral: the House represents people by population; the Senate gives every state equal representation. Together they make federal law.

435

House of Representatives

435 voting members, apportioned among states by population.

  • Two-year terms.
  • All 435 voting seats are contested every two years.
  • Each member represents a congressional district.
  • Minimum age 25; US citizen for 7 years; inhabitant of the state represented.
  • Speaker elected by House members.

US Senate

Two Senators from each of the 50 states, regardless of population.

  • Six-year terms.
  • Terms are staggered; roughly one-third of seats are contested every two years.

  • Vice President is President of the Senate; a President pro tempore presides in their absence.

Midterm elections

Midterms occur halfway through a presidential four-year term. Voters elect all House seats, roughly one-third of Senate seats, and many state/local offices. Midterms do not elect a new President.

ELECTION CYCLE · CONGRESS · PRESIDENTIAL ACCOUNTABILITY

US midterm elections — what they are and why they matter

A practical guide to the difference between a presidential election and a midterm election, what voters elect, how Congress can change hands, and how that affects a President's ability to govern.

Start with the simplest explanation

Presidential election

Voters choose the President for a four-year term. It takes place every four years.

Midterm election

Two years after a presidential election, voters elect the entire House and roughly one-third of the Senate. The sitting President is not being elected again in this election.

Important wording: “Full-term election” is not the usual official name of a US election. A presidential term lasts four years. The election that chooses a President is called a presidential election; the election halfway through that term is called a midterm election.

Why “midterm”? It occurs around the middle of the President's four-year period: two years after one presidential election and two years before the next.

2. What do Americans vote for in a midterm?

House of Representatives
435

All voting seats are contested every two years. Representatives serve two-year terms.

Senate
About ⅓

Senators serve six-year terms. Seats are divided into three classes, so normally about one-third are regularly contested every two years.

State & local offices
Varies

Many states also elect governors, state legislators and local officials. Ballots differ by state and year.

The President is not on the midterm ballot for a new presidential term. The Vice President is not separately elected in a midterm either; the two run together in a presidential election.

3. Why does the US hold midterm elections?

  1. Accountability: voters can express approval or disapproval of the administration before the next presidential election.
  2. Regular representation: every House seat is subject to election every two years.
  3. Senate continuity: staggered six-year terms mean the whole Senate is not replaced at once.
  4. Checks and balances: voters can elect a Congress controlled by a different party from the President's party.
Midterms are often treated politically as a referendum on the President, but legally they are elections for congressional, state and local offices. Losing a midterm is not a vote of no confidence that removes the President.

4. How can midterms change political power?

The President heads the executive branch. Congress is the federal legislature. A midterm can change the party balance in Congress without changing the President or the executive branch.

6. Can a midterm election remove the President?

No. Losing a midterm does not remove a President, shorten the term or trigger a new presidential election. The President normally remains in office until the term ends.

8. Does the President's party usually lose midterms?

Historically, the party holding the presidency has often lost House seats in midterms. This is sometimes called the midterm penalty.

  • Supporters of the winning President may be less motivated to vote again two years later.
  • Opposition voters may be more motivated to limit the President's power.
  • Economic conditions, inflation, wars, controversies and presidential approval can affect voting.
  • Candidate quality, district boundaries and which Senate seats are up also matter.
It is a tendency, not a rule. The President's party can gain seats or retain control.

9. A simple example

Suppose a Republican President wins a presidential election. Two years later, voters elect a Democratic majority in both chambers.

  • The Republican President remains President for the rest of the term.
  • The Democratic Congress can pass bills, conduct oversight and negotiate the federal budget.
  • The President can veto bills; Congress can override a veto only with two-thirds votes in both chambers.
  • Both sides may need to compromise to enact legislation.
  • Voters choose the next President at the next presidential election.
Core mental model: Presidential elections decide who leads the executive branch. Midterms can change who controls Congress. The President and Congress have separate mandates and constitutional powers.

How to read these tables

  • Constitutional/legal power means authority granted by the Constitution or statute; it is not unlimited personal power.
  • Practical influence means the ability to shape agendas, persuade colleagues, manage departments or influence public debate.

A Senator is not a mini-President

A Senator has one vote in the Senate, legislative and oversight powers, and influence through committees and leadership. A Senator cannot command federal agencies, deploy troops or enact a law alone.

A President is powerful, not absolute

The President leads the executive branch and commands the armed forces, but Congress controls legislation and appropriations, the Senate confirms many nominees and treaties, and courts decide legal cases.

A Supreme Court Justice is not a policymaker by office

Justices decide cases and controversies under law. Their rulings can have major policy consequences, but they do not run departments or introduce legislation.

Governors are not federal officers

Governors lead state governments under their own state constitutions. Their powers vary; they do not report to the President in ordinary state matters, although federal law and the Constitution remain supreme.

    State governments · Governor, legislature & courts

    Each US state has its own constitution, executive, legislature and judicial system. States have substantial authority over education, policing, elections, public health, transport and many other areas, subject to the US Constitution and federal law.

    Who is elected at state level?

    Governor

    Chief executive of the state; usually elected statewide, commonly for four years (term rules vary).

    State legislators

    Represent districts in a state House/Assembly and, in most states, state Senate.

    Attorney general, secretary of state, treasurer, auditor and judges may be elected or appointed depending on state.

    49 states have two legislative chambers. Nebraska is the exception: it has a single-chamber, officially nonpartisan legislature. State upper houses are not the US Senate; they are part of the state government.

    100 Senators: two from each state. Makes federal law in Washington, D.C.

    State Senate

    Part of an individual state's legislature. Membership and districts vary by state.

    POLITICAL PARTIES

    Parties, primaries and the political landscape

    The US has multiple parties, but national elections are dominated by two major parties. The Constitution does not establish or require a two-party system.

    Democratic Party

    One of the two major US political parties. Often associated with centre-left / liberal positions in contemporary national politics, though its coalition is diverse and views vary by issue and region.

    Republican Party

    One of the two major US political parties. Often associated with centre-right / conservative positions in contemporary national politics, though its coalition is diverse and views vary by issue and region.

    Other parties and independents

    Libertarian, Green and other parties, as well as independent candidates, may appear on ballots. Ballot access rules are state-specific. Third-party candidates can affect vote distribution, but they rarely win the presidency under the current Electoral College and winner-take-all state arrangements.

    Why do parties have primaries?

    In a parliamentary system such as India, parties also select candidates internally, but the US presidential primary system is a highly visible, state-by-state public contest to decide who will represent a party nationally. The details are governed by party rules and state law.

    Party affiliation is not the same as constitutional office. A Senator or Governor is elected to a public office; the party is the political organisation with which that candidate is affiliated.

    Important historical facts

    • George Washington (1st): first President; served two terms and declined to seek a third.
    • Abraham Lincoln (16th): led the Union during the Civil War; issued the Emancipation Proclamation and supported the 13th Amendment ending slavery.
    • Franklin D. Roosevelt (32nd): served four elected terms; the 22nd Amendment later established the general two-election limit.
    • John F. Kennedy (35th): youngest person elected President; Theodore Roosevelt was younger when he first assumed the office, but was not elected President at that point.
    • Gerald Ford (38th): became President without being elected President or Vice President, following Nixon's resignation; he had been appointed Vice President under the 25th Amendment.
    • Barack Obama (44th): first African American President.
    • Grover Cleveland (22nd and 24th): only President with two non-consecutive terms before Donald Trump.
    • Donald Trump (45th and 47th): second President elected to non-consecutive terms.

    BILATERAL HISTORY · DIPLOMACY · SECURITY · TRADE

    India–United States relations: 1947–2026

    A detailed, non-linear relationship: democratic affinity and strategic convergence coexist with disagreements over Pakistan, China, Russia, nuclear policy, trade, technology, immigration and human rights. Neither country is a treaty ally of the other; cooperation is issue-based and shaped by national interests.

    Why the relationship matters

    India offers scale, a large market, technology talent, Indian Ocean geography and a major role in the Indo-Pacific. The United States offers capital, advanced technology, defence systems, education, a large diaspora network and global financial reach.

    Why friction keeps returning

    India values strategic autonomy and resists alliance obligations; the US often seeks alignment on sanctions, export controls, Russia and China. Trade access, tariffs, visas, data rules, agricultural standards and intellectual-property disputes repeatedly create tension.

    How to read this history

    Each administration is assessed by actions and outcomes, not party labels. A friendly summit does not mean every policy was favourable to India; a dispute does not mean the whole relationship failed. Separate public rhetoric, signed agreements, actual implementation and long-term consequences.

    Presidential administration timeline

    1947–1953 · Harry S. Truman

    Recognition and early engagement

    Cooperation / positive developments: The United States recognised independent India on 15 August 1947; diplomatic relations had been established in 1946. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru visited Washington in 1949.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: India’s non-alignment and US Cold War priorities limited strategic alignment. Washington increasingly built a security relationship with Pakistan, which New Delhi viewed with concern.

    Overall reading: Foundation of diplomatic ties; democratic partnership language existed, but strategic expectations differed.

    1953–1961 · Dwight D. Eisenhower

    Food aid, Pakistan alignment and first presidential visit

    Cooperation / positive developments: Eisenhower visited India in December 1959, the first sitting US president to do so. US food assistance, including PL-480 supplies, supported India during shortages.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: US military ties with Pakistan through Cold War security pacts troubled India. Washington’s regional policy was often viewed in Delhi as tilted toward Pakistan.

    Overall reading: High-level goodwill and development assistance alongside a serious security trust deficit.

    1961–1963 · John F. Kennedy

    1962 China war: major US support

    Cooperation / positive developments: After China attacked India in 1962, Kennedy’s administration provided emergency military supplies, logistics and intelligence support.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: Support was crisis-driven and did not become a formal alliance. US engagement with Pakistan remained a source of Indian concern.

    Overall reading: One of the clearest early episodes of US support during a direct security crisis for India.

    1963–1969 · Lyndon B. Johnson

    Food diplomacy and 1965 war tensions

    Cooperation / positive developments: US food shipments helped India manage food insecurity; the Green Revolution era received important international support.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: During the 1965 India–Pakistan war, the US suspended arms supplies to both countries. India viewed the embargo and US crisis diplomacy as inadequate; relations cooled.

    Overall reading: Development cooperation continued, but security alignment remained limited.

    1969–1974 · Richard Nixon

    1971 rupture: the most severe Cold War low point

    Cooperation / positive developments: The US sought to preserve its relationship with Pakistan, which was a channel to China. The 1972–73 period eventually returned to pragmatic diplomacy.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: During the 1971 Bangladesh crisis and war, Nixon and Henry Kissinger tilted toward Pakistan despite mass atrocities in East Pakistan; the US dispatched Task Force 74, including USS Enterprise, into the Bay of Bengal. India saw this as coercive signalling.

    Overall reading: A defining negative chapter. It entrenched Indian distrust of US South Asia policy for years.

    1974–1977 · Gerald Ford

    Nuclear test aftermath

    Cooperation / positive developments: The US continued diplomatic engagement after India’s 1974 “peaceful nuclear explosion” at Pokhran.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: Nuclear non-proliferation concerns increasingly restricted nuclear cooperation and technology access.

    Overall reading: The nuclear issue became a long-term structural source of friction.

    1977–1981 · Jimmy Carter

    Non-proliferation dispute and presidential visit

    Cooperation / positive developments: Carter visited India in 1978; both sides discussed democracy, development, energy and global issues.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: US nuclear non-proliferation law and concerns over India’s nuclear programme complicated fuel supply to the Tarapur reactors and broader cooperation.

    Overall reading: Warm leadership engagement, but hard disagreement over nuclear policy.

    1981–1989 · Ronald Reagan

    Gradual strategic reopening

    Cooperation / positive developments: Dialogue expanded and the relationship became more pragmatic; scientific, educational and commercial contacts grew.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: US military and intelligence cooperation with Pakistan during the Soviet–Afghan war, including support to the Afghan mujahideen, reinforced Indian concerns about Pakistan’s military capacity and regional balance.

    Overall reading: Pragmatic engagement constrained by Afghanistan and Pakistan.

    1989–1993 · George H. W. Bush

    Post-Cold War transition

    Cooperation / positive developments: The end of the Cold War created an opportunity to redefine ties beyond the old bloc framework.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: India faced pressure over economic and nuclear policy; strategic convergence was still limited and the relationship did not yet have a modern partnership architecture.

    Overall reading: A transitional period rather than a breakthrough.

    1993–2001 · Bill Clinton

    Nuclear sanctions, then historic reset

    Cooperation / positive developments: After the 1998 nuclear tests, sustained diplomacy led by officials including Strobe Talbott and Jaswant Singh helped rebuild trust. Clinton visited India in March 2000, a landmark presidential visit.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: The Clinton administration imposed sanctions after India’s 1998 tests (and also after Pakistan’s tests); technology and defence cooperation were restricted. US criticism of the 1998 tests was a major bilateral rupture.

    Overall reading: From sanctions and isolation toward a strategic reset; a major turning point.

    2001–2009 · George W. Bush

    Civil nuclear deal and strategic partnership

    Cooperation / positive developments: The 2005 civil nuclear initiative and 2008 NSG waiver transformed nuclear engagement; defence, technology and counterterrorism ties expanded. The 2005 framework established a strategic partnership.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: The deal faced strong non-proliferation criticism and domestic opposition in both countries. Implementation did not produce the scale of commercial nuclear power cooperation initially anticipated.

    Overall reading: One of the most consequential positive periods in modern relations.

    2009–2017 · Barack Obama

    Defence partnership, Indo-Pacific and climate cooperation

    Cooperation / positive developments: Obama visited India in 2010 and 2015; the US recognised India as a Major Defense Partner in 2016. Logistics and technology cooperation advanced; the two sides deepened the Quad dialogue.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: Trade disputes, visa and outsourcing debates, intellectual-property concerns and differences over climate and Russia remained. The US also criticised some Indian domestic policies and human-rights developments.

    Overall reading: Strategic convergence accelerated, but not without commercial and values-based friction.

    2017–2021 · Donald Trump (45th)

    Indo-Pacific alignment with trade and tariff disputes

    Cooperation / positive developments: Defence cooperation and Quad coordination strengthened; COMCASA was signed in 2018 and BECA in 2020; leaders cultivated a high-profile political relationship.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: The US ended India’s GSP trade preference in 2019; tariffs and market-access disputes intensified. CAATSA-related pressure over India’s S-400 purchase and H-1B visa restrictions created friction.

    Overall reading: Security ties strengthened while trade and immigration disputes remained sharp.

    2021–2025 · Joe Biden

    iCET, critical technology and defence co-production

    Cooperation / positive developments: The Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology (iCET), 2023 state visit, defence industrial cooperation, semiconductor and space collaboration, and Quad work deepened the technology partnership.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: India’s continued purchase of Russian arms and energy, differences over the Ukraine war, human-rights concerns and allegations involving plots against Sikh separatists in the US created serious diplomatic strain.

    Overall reading: Broad strategic convergence, with trust tested by Russia and transnational security concerns.

    2025–2026 · Donald Trump (47th) · ongoing

    Tariff pressure, negotiation and a new trade framework

    Cooperation / positive developments: The two governments launched broader Bilateral Trade Agreement negotiations in February 2025. A US–India joint statement on 6 February 2026 announced a framework for an interim reciprocal trade agreement, including an 18% US reciprocal tariff rate on specified Indian-origin goods and prospective tariff adjustments subject to completion.

    Conflict / criticism / negative developments: The 2025 tariff measures and trade pressure created uncertainty for exporters and bilateral trust. Differences over energy purchases, market access, immigration and strategic autonomy remain. The February 2026 statement is a framework; do not treat every proposed concession as fully implemented without checking subsequent legal notices and implementation updates.

    Overall reading: A transactional phase: strategic cooperation continues, but trade leverage and tariff policy are central sources of friction. This administration is ongoing as of 4 November 2026.

    Native peoples: important historical and political context

    • Indigenous peoples were present across the continent long before European colonisation; there is no single “Native American” nation, language or culture.
    • Tribal nations have distinct governments, citizenship rules, territories, treaties and relationships with federal and state governments.
    • Federal Indian policy included treaty-making, forced removal, reservation confinement, allotment, boarding-school assimilation and termination-era policies; later periods included self-determination legislation and restoration of tribal authority.
    • The Indian Removal Act (1830), Trail of Tears, Dawes Act (1887), Indian Citizenship Act (1924), Indian Reorganization Act (1934), termination policies and Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975) are major milestones to study.
    • AIAN alone counts are not a full count of everyone with Indigenous ancestry. For broad identity, consult “alone or in combination” tables and detailed tribal data.

    Immigration: what the numbers do and do not tell you

    • Naturalized citizen: foreign-born person who became a U.S. citizen through naturalization.
    • Lawful permanent resident (LPR): commonly called a green-card holder; may live and work permanently, subject to immigration law.
    • Nonimmigrant: temporary admission category such as student, exchange visitor, temporary worker or visitor.
    • Refugee / asylee: people granted protection under distinct legal processes.
    • Unauthorized population: not directly identified by the ACS as a legal-status category; estimates are produced by specialist agencies and research organisations using different methods.
    • Immigration pathways: family-sponsored, employment-based, diversity visa, humanitarian protection and other statutory categories. Visa availability, annual caps, country limits and processing backlogs differ by category.

    Do not interpret “foreign-born” as “illegal immigrant”. The ACS foreign-born figure includes naturalized U.S. citizens and many lawful residents, as well as people whose status is not lawful.

    Important interpretation notes

    • Permanent resident status is not a visa category: a Green Card holder is an LPR. A person can become an LPR through an immigrant visa issued abroad or through adjustment of status inside the United States.
    • Immigrant visa issued does not always equal LPR granted in the same year: issuance, travel and admission can occur at different times. Some LPRs never receive an immigrant visa abroad because they adjust status domestically.
    • Nonimmigrant visas are temporary classifications: a visa may allow a person to seek admission for a purpose and period, but the visa itself does not guarantee entry or determine the length of stay.
    • Counts are not unique-person totals across years: the same person may receive more than one visa or make multiple trips. Do not add visa issuances to LPR grants to estimate the number of immigrants.

    REFERENCE DESK

    Glossary & frequently asked questions

    Plain-language definitions for the terms that commonly cause confusion when comparing US and Indian politics.

    Glossary

    Electoral College

    The constitutional process in which appointed electors cast votes for President and Vice President.

    Elector

    A person appointed under state law to cast an Electoral College vote.

    Electoral vote

    A vote cast by an elector; distinct from a citizen's popular vote.

    Popular vote

    Votes cast by individual voters in an election.

    Primary

    A party nomination contest, usually using a secret ballot.

    Caucus

    A party-run meeting used in the nomination process.

    Delegate

    A person selected to represent a state or group at a party's national convention.

    Convention

    A party gathering that formally nominates its presidential ticket and adopts its platform.

    Midterm

    Congressional and other elections held halfway through a President's four-year term.

    Congressional district

    A geographic constituency that elects one voting member of the House.

    Federalism

    Constitutional division of governmental authority between national and state governments.

    Divided government

    When different parties control the presidency and one or both chambers of Congress.

    Contingent election

    Constitutional fallback where the House selects the President and the Senate selects the Vice President if no candidate wins an Electoral College majority.

    Canvass

    Official process of aggregating, reconciling and reviewing vote totals.

    Certification

    Formal attestation of election results by the authority designated under law.

    FEC

    Federal Election Commission; administers and enforces federal campaign-finance law.

    ECI

    Election Commission of India; constitutional body responsible for specified national and state elections.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does the US have 50 states or 52?

    It has 50 states. Washington, D.C. is a federal district, not a state. Puerto Rico and other territories are not states either.

    Does Washington, D.C. vote for President?

    Yes. Under the 23rd Amendment, D.C. has three Electoral College electors. It has no voting Senator and has a non-voting delegate in the House.

    Why are there 538 electoral votes?

    435 House seats + 100 Senate seats + 3 electors for D.C. = 538.

    Why does a small state have two Senators just like California?

    The Senate was designed to give states equal representation in one chamber, while the House represents population proportionally.

    Can the President be from one party while Congress is controlled by another?

    Yes. Presidential and congressional elections are separate. This can create divided government.

    Is the US Senate the same as Rajya Sabha?

    They are both upper chambers, but their composition and federal logic differ. Every US state has two Senators; Rajya Sabha representation is allocated by population and members are mainly elected by MLAs.

    Is the US Governor like an Indian Governor?

    No. A US state Governor is usually directly elected and is the state's chief executive. An Indian state Governor is appointed by the President and ordinarily acts on the advice of the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers.

    Does the US have a Prime Minister?

    No. The US President leads the federal executive and is both head of state and head of government.

    Can a President win more popular votes but lose?

    Yes. The constitutional result is based on Electoral College votes allocated state by state, not a single nationwide popular-vote total.

    What happens if no one reaches 270?

    The 12th Amendment provides a contingent election: the House chooses the President and the Senate chooses the Vice President.
