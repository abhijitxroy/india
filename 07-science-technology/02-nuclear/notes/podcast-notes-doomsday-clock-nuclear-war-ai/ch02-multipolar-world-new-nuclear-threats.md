# 02 — Multipolar World & New Nuclear Threats

**Timestamp:** 06:29  
**Source chapter:** *Multipolar World & New Nuclear Threats*

## 1. From bipolar to multipolar nuclear politics

The discussion divides the nuclear age into broad phases.

### Cold War / bipolar period

The major nuclear confrontation was between:

- United States
- Soviet Union

The world was described as bipolar because the two superpowers dominated the strategic nuclear balance.

The basic geopolitical logic was:

- If a country was aligned against the United States, it could potentially align with the Soviet Union.
- If it was against the Soviet Union, it could potentially align with the United States.

### Post-Cold War period

After the Soviet Union collapsed, the United States became the dominant global power.

During this period:

- India became a nuclear power.
- Pakistan became a nuclear power.
- North Korea developed nuclear weapons.
- China continued to rise economically and strategically.

### Current multipolar period

The discussion argues that the world is now genuinely multipolar.

The major nuclear powers discussed include:

- United States
- Russia
- China
- India
- Pakistan
- North Korea
- Israel, described by the speaker as possessing nuclear capability despite Israel's policy of ambiguity.

Russia is described in the discussion as having the world's largest nuclear stockpile, while China is described as having accumulated a large and growing number of warheads.

## 2. Why arms control appears weaker

The speaker contrasts the current period with the earlier emphasis on:

- Arms control.
- Nuclear-weapons control.
- Treaties.
- No-first-use concepts.
- Nuclear testing restrictions.

The argument is that these mechanisms are losing influence because major powers are increasingly unwilling to accept rules that they themselves do not consistently follow.

The speaker summarizes this as a credibility problem:

> A country cannot effectively demand restrictions from others while refusing comparable restrictions for itself.

The discussion characterizes this imbalance as a form of strategic bullying.

## 3. Changing global power structure

The rise of China and the resurgence of Russia are presented as major changes.

The discussion says:

- China has become a serious rival to the United States.
- Russia remains the largest nuclear stockpiler according to the speaker.
- Russia under Vladimir Putin is described as pursuing a new nationalist strategic direction.
- India has become an important rising economic and nuclear power.
- The international system no longer has one uncontested “king.”

The competition is therefore increasingly about relative dominance among several major powers.

The speaker also connects this competition to challenges against the US dollar and the petrodollar system.

## 4. The North Korea lesson

North Korea is used as an example of the perceived strategic lesson of nuclear weapons.

The argument is:

1. North Korea developed nuclear weapons.
2. It also developed missiles capable of delivering them.
3. Once it became a nuclear-armed state, major powers became more cautious about directly confronting it.
4. Other countries could therefore interpret nuclearization as a way of obtaining strategic protection.

This creates a dangerous incentive structure.

The speaker argues that the lesson could encourage additional countries to pursue nuclear weapons.

## 5. Potential nuclear proliferation

The discussion mentions:

- Iran.
- Brazil.
- South Africa.
- Israel.

The speaker argues that nuclear technology tends to spread over time, similar to other technologies.

The analogy used is that technologies once restricted to a few advanced countries eventually become available to many countries.

Therefore, the world should prepare for a future in which more states may possess nuclear capabilities.

## 6. Why coercion may not work

The speaker argues that a future nuclear order cannot simply be imposed through force.

Instead:

- Countries will need negotiated arrangements.
- Nuclear states need to recognize the concerns of other states.
- Agreements must be reciprocal.
- A rules-based system must be credible to all participants.

The proposed direction is therefore a **deal-based nuclear regime rather than a force-based regime**.

## 7. Nuclear threats beyond conventional nuclear weapons

The discussion warns that the danger is not limited to full-scale nuclear war.

Other threats include:

- Dirty bombs.
- Radiation-emitting devices.
- Smaller nuclear or radiological devices.
- Terrorist acquisition of radioactive or nuclear material.

A dirty bomb is described as primarily a **radiological weapon**, where radioactive contamination is the central danger rather than a nuclear explosion.

The chapter concludes that a new nuclear security regime must account for these lower-scale threats as well.
