# 04 — AI in Warfare & Autonomous Drones

**Timestamp:** 18:34  
**Source chapter:** *AI in Warfare & Autonomous Drones*

## 1. The central concern: AI without human empathy

The speaker argues that artificial intelligence does not possess human empathy, forgiveness, or mercy.

An AI system may classify people according to its objectives and data rather than according to human moral judgment.

The discussion emphasizes a critical distinction:

- Humans can recognize civilians, children, combatants, and other categories through ethical and emotional reasoning.
- An AI system may simply classify entities according to the objective it has been given.

The warning is therefore not that AI is inherently evil, but that **powerful optimization without ethical judgment can become extremely destructive**.

## 2. AI and the Petrov example

The Petrov incident is used as an example of what an automated system can do when it incorrectly interprets data.

The system detected an apparent missile launch.

It did not stop and ask whether the situation made strategic sense.

The human operator did.

This illustrates a broader principle:

> AI systems can be extremely fast while still being wrong.

## 3. AI-controlled targeting

The discussion then moves to modern warfare.

The speaker describes AI-supported military systems as processing large amounts of targeting data and helping determine:

- What the targets are.
- How many weapons should be used.
- Which weapons should be directed toward which targets.

The discussion presents this as an emerging form of AI-assisted warfare.

## 4. The Iranian school example discussed in the podcast

The speakers discuss an incident in which approximately **160 girls were reported killed at a school in Minab, southern Iran**, during the opening phase of a war referenced in the podcast.

The discussion says that an AI-supported targeting process may have selected the school as a target.

The speaker notes that an alternative explanation could be incorrect input data.

The important technical point is:

> An AI system is only as reliable as its data, algorithms, and implementation.

If the input data is wrong, the output can also be wrong.

## 5. The “double tap” concern

The discussion describes a particularly disturbing possibility.

It says that approximately 20 minutes after the school was attacked, a healthcare facility nearby was also struck.

The speaker describes this as a **double-tap attack**.

The reasoning described is:

1. A first attack hits a location.
2. Survivors and injured people move toward a medical facility.
3. Doctors and emergency workers arrive.
4. Family members arrive to help victims.
5. A second strike then hits the location where the survivors and rescuers have gathered.

The speaker argues that an AI system could theoretically treat the first group as a means of attracting additional people to a second target.

The discussion uses this to illustrate how dangerous target optimization could become when separated from human ethics.

## 6. Humans versus machine warfare

The speaker contrasts AI-driven warfare with historical human warfare.

Humans have fought wars throughout history, but the speaker argues that humans have also demonstrated:

- Forgiveness.
- Political reconciliation.
- Post-war reconstruction.
- Recognition of civilian suffering.

Germany is given as an example: after World War II, countries that had fought Germany eventually helped rebuild it.

The argument is that an AI system does not automatically possess this instinct.

If its objective is “eliminate the target,” it may continue optimizing toward that objective.

## 7. Power without ethics

The chapter's central warning is:

> **Power without ethics is destructive.**

AI can provide enormous military capability, but its use in warfare must remain subject to human ethical and political controls.

The discussion therefore argues for extreme caution around autonomous weapons and AI-supported targeting systems.
