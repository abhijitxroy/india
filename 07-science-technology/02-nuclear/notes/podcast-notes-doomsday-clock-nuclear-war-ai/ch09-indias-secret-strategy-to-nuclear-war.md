# 09 — India's Secret Strategy to Nuclear War

**Timestamp:** 35:57  
**Source chapter:** *India's Secret Strategy to Nuclear War*

## 1. India's hard power

The discussion argues that India has developed substantial military capability in response to the global strategic environment.

The speaker describes India's missile technology as among the best in the world.

## 2. BrahMos versus Tomahawk

The speaker compares India's **BrahMos** cruise missile with the US **Tomahawk**.

According to the discussion:

- BrahMos is faster.
- BrahMos is more accurate.
- The speaker claims approximately five times the accuracy of Tomahawk.
- It is described as traveling at roughly twice the speed.
- Its cost is described as about 65% of Tomahawk's.
- India's production capability is described as approximately 1.5 times the US production rate of Tomahawk.

The discussion also mentions expanding BrahMos production in India, including facilities associated with Uttar Pradesh and Nagpur.

## 3. Agni and MIRV capability

The speaker describes the **Agni** missile family as one of India's major strategic missile capabilities.

A range of approximately **5,000–8,000 km** is mentioned.

The discussion focuses particularly on **MIRV — Multiple Independently targetable Reentry Vehicle** capability.

The speaker distinguishes MIRV from cluster munitions.

### Cluster munition

The discussion describes a cluster weapon as breaking apart and releasing multiple submunitions.

### MIRV

A MIRV-equipped ballistic missile can release multiple reentry vehicles that can be directed toward different targets.

The strategic advantage described is that a defender may have difficulty determining:

- How many reentry vehicles will separate.
- Where each will go.
- Which targets they are intended to hit.

The speaker describes India's MIRV capability as primarily a deterrent against China rather than Pakistan.

The speaker also says such missiles may have nuclear or conventional applications.

## 4. India's submarine and naval capability

The discussion highlights:

- Submarine capability.
- Aircraft carriers.
- Nuclear deterrence.
- Survivable launch platforms.

These form part of India's broader strategic force.

## 5. Low-cost and high-production weapons

The speaker emphasizes that modern warfare is not only about expensive strategic weapons.

India is also described as investing in:

- Low-cost weapons.
- High-production systems.
- Drones.
- Loitering munitions.

The discussion compares India's **Sheshnaag** drone with Iran/Russia-associated Shahed-type drones.

The speaker describes Sheshnaag as an indigenous Indian technology.

## 6. What is a loitering munition?

A loitering munition combines characteristics of:

- A drone.
- A munition.

It can:

1. Fly around an area.
2. Remain over or near a target.
3. Wait for an opportunity.
4. Attack.
5. Be destroyed as part of the attack.

The key advantage is that it does not have to attack immediately after launch.

## 7. Operation Sindoor example

The discussion references India's **Operation Sindoor** and says the capabilities publicly demonstrated were only part of India's available military capability.

The speaker argues that India could have used much more powerful systems but deliberately avoided escalating the conflict.

One example given is that India could theoretically have used an Agni launch against multiple targets, but chose not to because Pakistan might interpret such a launch as a nuclear attack.

The principle expressed is:

> Use the minimum level of force necessary to accomplish the objective.

The speaker summarizes this through the metaphor of using a needle when a needle is sufficient and a sword only when a sword is required.

## 8. Hardware versus the ability to use it

The speaker makes an important distinction:

**Having weapons is one thing. Being able to use them effectively is another.**

Military strength therefore depends on:

- Weapons.
- Production.
- Targeting.
- Command.
- Training.
- Operational doctrine.
- Political decision-making.

## 9. India's soft power

The speaker argues that India's **soft power may be even more important than its hard power**.

Iran is used as an example.

The argument is that India has not historically treated Iran as an enemy and has not continuously interfered in Iranian affairs.

Therefore, during regional conflict, Indian interests can sometimes receive different treatment because India maintains relationships with multiple sides.

This is presented as a strategic advantage:

> India can maintain relationships with countries that may be adversaries of one another.

## 10. Overall strategic model

The chapter presents India's strength as a combination of:

- Military hardware.
- Nuclear deterrence.
- Missile technology.
- Submarine survivability.
- Drones.
- Production capacity.
- Operational restraint.
- Political will.
- Diplomatic relationships.
- Soft power.

The underlying idea is that strategic strength is not simply the number of weapons a country owns; it is the ability to combine military capability with restraint and diplomacy.
