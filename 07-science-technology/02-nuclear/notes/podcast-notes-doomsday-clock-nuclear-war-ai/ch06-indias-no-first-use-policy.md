# 06 — India's No First Use Policy

**Timestamp:** 22:58  
**Source chapter:** *India's No First Use Policy*

## 1. What No First Use means

A **No First Use (NFU)** policy means that a country declares that it will not be the first to use nuclear weapons against another country.

The discussion argues that NFU should not automatically be interpreted as weakness.

Instead, it can reduce uncertainty and the possibility of accidental escalation.

## 2. Why NFU can reduce confusion

The Petrov incident is used as an example.

If two nuclear powers have a credible no-first-use understanding, a warning indicating an incoming missile attack can be interpreted differently.

A commander can ask:

> Why would the other side launch a nuclear first strike if it has explicitly promised not to do so?

This additional political context can provide valuable time to investigate a suspicious warning.

The discussion argues that such a principle could have reduced the risk associated with a false warning such as the 1983 Oko incident.

## 3. India's argument for NFU

The speaker presents India's NFU approach as a combination of:

- Political restraint.
- Strategic confidence.
- Retaliatory capability.
- Reduced ambiguity.

The policy is not described as surrendering the ability to retaliate.

Instead, the message is:

> India will not start the nuclear exchange, but if India is attacked, the attacker should expect devastating retaliation.

## 4. India's survivable retaliation

The discussion argues that India's nuclear force is sufficiently capable that an attacker cannot assume it can eliminate India's ability to retaliate.

The speaker specifically highlights India's **nuclear triad**:

- Land-based systems.
- Air-launched systems.
- Submarine-based systems.

The submarine component is particularly important because submarines can remain hidden at sea.

Even if an enemy attacks known land-based missile sites and air bases, it may not know where India's nuclear submarines are.

This gives India an assured second-strike capability according to the discussion.

## 5. India, Pakistan and China

The speaker applies the same deterrence logic to both Pakistan and China.

Against Pakistan, the discussion emphasizes India's larger territory, larger arsenal as described by the speaker, and thermonuclear capability.

Against China, the argument focuses on survivability.

Even if China attempted to destroy India's land-based missile sites and air bases, hidden submarines could survive and retaliate.

## 6. Why satellites matter

The discussion also highlights satellite-based detection.

The argument is that India can detect incoming launches and respond rapidly.

Combined with:

- Nuclear weapons.
- A triad.
- Submarine survivability.
- Early-warning capabilities.

NFU is presented as compatible with strong deterrence.

## 7. The philosophical argument

The speaker connects this restraint to an Indian philosophical principle:

> Greater power should produce greater responsibility.

The message is not “we cannot fight.”

It is:

> We will not attack you first, but if you attack us, you should understand that you will not survive the retaliation.

## 8. The proposed global model

The speaker argues that NFU should eventually become a global principle.

The proposed arrangement would involve:

- India.
- United States.
- Russia.
- Other nuclear powers.

All agreeing that nuclear weapons will not be used first.

The speaker presents this as a possible way to reduce strategic confusion and make the world safer.
